Tea Ceramics

Art

Tea ceramics are a history of “how tea is drunk” shaping “how vessels are made”: Tang Yue celadon and Xing white ware, Song Jian black bowls and Longquan celadon, Ming Yixing stoneware, Qing export porcelain — each matches a tea method and aesthetic. Tea vessels are not antique display but a union of function, material, firing and beauty. Treating Jian bowls as the highest standard for all tea ware ignores changes in tea practice.

Tang: celadon and white ware

Tang boiled tea used bowls, grinders, sieves, cauldrons, ladles and bamboo whisks. Yue celadon was likened to jade and ice, Xing white ware to silver and snow; they were the two great bowl systems. Lu Yu's Classic says Yue celadon is blue-green and enhances tea's green, while Xing white makes tea look red. This is not absolute colour theory but Tang experience of liquor and glaze.

Tang bowls were broad, easy to ladle and drink from; slanted walls helped powder and water mix. Celadon echoed the green liquor; white ware set off red-brown. The “jade/ice/silver/snow” metaphors show tea ware already tied to colour, touch and status.

Song: black bowls and celadon

Song whisked tea valued white, and Jian black bowls set off white foam. Jian kilns in Jianyang, Fujian, specialised in tea bowls; hare's fur, oil spot and yohen are iron-glaze crystal/liquid-phase effects at high temperature. Cai Xiang's Record of Tea says “tea is white, so black bowls suit it”; Jian'an ware is “dark purple-black, patterned like hare's fur,” with a thick body that holds heat.

Song also had Longquan celadon, Jingdezhen qingbai, Jizhou and Ding ware. Celadon suited plain drinking, whisking and literati taste; white showed liquor colour; Jizhou's leaf and paper-cut decoration brought folk motifs into tea bowls. “Sky after rain,” “ice crackle” and “hare's fur” are not one standard but different kilns, glazes and firing atmospheres.

Ming: Yixing and white porcelain

Ming abolished cakes and promoted leaf; direct infusion made the teapot central. Yixing stoneware uses local clay, breathes, holds heat and does not steal aroma, suiting oolong, pu'er and black tea; Gongchun and Shi Dabin raised it from household ware to literati art. Clay, shape, capacity, pour and firing all affect taste.

Ming also used white, celadon and blue-and-white cups and bowls; white showed liquor, blue-and-white matched literati taste. Tea ware shifted from grinder, sieve and whisk to pot, cup and stove; “smallness” became aesthetic — small pot, small cup, small stove for control of strength and temperature.

Qing and export

Qing tea ware diversified: Yixing, blue-and-white, famille rose, wucai and export porcelain. European markets wanted blue-and-white and famille rose tea sets; Chinese export ware was customised to Western shapes, creating “Chinoiserie” tea ware. Qing gongfu used small pots and cups, Yixing and white porcelain together; teahouses used large pots and gaiwan for practicality and durability.

Japanese chanoyu absorbed Jian bowls as “tenmoku” for matcha; Korea absorbed celadon and white ware into its own bowl system. Cross-cultural tea ware is not “China exports, others copy” but each place reshaping shape, glaze and size for its own tea method.

Brewing and boundaries

Choose the vessel by tea: glass or white gaiwan for green tea to see leaf and control heat; Yixing or gaiwan for oolong to hold heat and aroma; porcelain pot/gaiwan for black tea; Yixing or clay for pu'er; Jian/matcha bowl and chasen for matcha. Capacity must match leaf: a small pot with much leaf overbrews; a large pot with little leaf underbrews.

Tea ware is not “older and more expensive is better”; antique ware may have lead glaze, damage or be unsuitable for daily use. Jian “yohen” is a glaze marvel, not a taste guarantee; Yixing “master” wares need authentication, and clay and workmanship matter more than the seal. Vessels are tools, not status symbols; function first, aesthetics second.
