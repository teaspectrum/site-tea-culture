Introduction

The Classic of Tea (Chajing) was written by Lu Yu in the late eighth century. It is arranged in three volumes and ten chapters, moving from the plant and its names to tools, processing, utensils, preparation, drinking, historical records and the tea regions of Tang China. It is often called the world’s first systematic book on tea, although that description is a modern evaluation rather than a Tang-era claim.

Structure

  • One: origins — the plant, its names and growing conditions.
  • Two: tools — equipment for picking and processing.
  • Three: making — seasonal timing and production steps.
  • Four: vessels — utensils for boiling and serving tea.
  • Five: boiling — roasting, grinding, water and seasoning.
  • Six: drinking — the history and manners of tea drinking.
  • Seven: records — documents and people.
  • Eight: producing regions — Tang-era tea areas.
  • Nine: simplification — which tools and steps may be omitted.
  • Ten: illustrations — pictures to display alongside the text.

Editions and reading

The surviving text is not Lu Yu’s manuscript but a tradition of copies and printed editions with textual variations. Modern editions usually add notes, vernacular translations and illustrations. These editorial layers have their own copyright and cannot be reproduced simply because the original text is in the public domain. Read the original alongside notes and a discussion of editions, and remember that Tang processing and preparation differ greatly from modern practice.

Why it matters

The Classic of Tea is important not only as a record of Tang tea practice. It establishes a way of writing about tea that places it within geography, objects, craft, history and human life. Later tea books, local gazetteers and cultural writing build on that framework.

Ten chapters

The Classic of Tea has ten chapters: origins (character, sources, regions); tools for picking and processing; production; vessels for boiling; boiling water and tea; drinking history and methods; tea lore; regions; simplification; and illustrations.

Craft and institutions

It records Tang cake-tea production: pick, steam, pound, press, roast, string and seal; boiling uses a cauldron, salt and bamboo whisk, with attention to boil stage and foam. It designs twenty-four vessels, turning tea into a procedure, and records tribute, tax, markets and regional teas — an encyclopedia of Tang tea.

Boundaries

It is not evidence that “Lu Yu invented tea”; tea existed before him. Nor is it a modern tea standard; Tang cake tea, boiling and salt differ from today’s leaf infusion. Read it by separating text, commentary, legend and later attribution; its value is the system, not the modern applicability of every parameter.

Lu Yu

陆羽（约 733–804）是唐代茶学家，著《茶经》，被后世尊为“茶圣”。他把茶的源流、采制、器具、煮饮、历史和产地系统化，设计二十四器，提出“山水上，江水中，井水下”等用水原则；他不是“发明茶的人”，而是把茶从地方经验整理成文化体系的人。

Pan-firing

Pan-firing is one step in the processing chain. Its position limits what the previous step left and what the next step can do; judging it outside the chain easily mistakes a process issue for a raw-material issue.
