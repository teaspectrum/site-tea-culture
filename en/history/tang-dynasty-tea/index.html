Tang Dynasty Tea

History

唐代是茶从地方饮品变成全国性文化的关键期：陆羽《茶经》系统总结采制、煮饮与器具，饼茶碾末、加盐煎煮成为主流，茶进入寺院、文人、宫廷和边疆贸易。唐代茶不是今天的散叶冲泡，而是碾末煎煮的“煎茶”。

The Tang was the period when tea moved from a regional drink to a national culture. Lu Yu's Classic of Tea systematised picking, processing, boiling and utensils; cake tea ground into powder and boiled with salt became mainstream; tea entered monasteries, literati circles, the court and frontier trade. Tang tea was not today's leaf infusion but “boiled tea” made from powdered cake.

From medicine to daily drink

Before the Tang, tea was often a medicine, tribute item or southern regional drink. In the Tang it became a national everyday beverage. The reasons were multiple: north-south transport, urban economy, monastic networks, exam-driven literati exchange and mature processing. Lu Yu (c. 733–804), in ten chapters, systematised tea's origins, processing, utensils, boiling, history and regions, and became the mother text for later tea books.

The Classic derives the character for tea from grass, wood or both, and traces Shennong, the Duke of Zhou and Yan Ying; these legends are partly invented, but they show Tang writers placing tea inside the Chinese cultural genealogy. Lu Yu also designed twenty-four vessels, turning tea boiling into a procedure rather than throwing leaves into a pot.

Boiled tea

The Tang mainstream was cake tea: leaves steamed, pounded, pressed into moulds and dried. To drink, the cake was roasted, ground and sieved. The boil went roughly: heat water to “fish-eye” bubbles, add salt, scoop out a ladle of water; add tea powder and stir with a bamboo whisk; when the water “surges like drums,” pour the reserved water back to calm it; ladle into bowls. The foam should be even — surface tension, not “the more bubbles the better.”

Tang also had loose, coarse and powdered tea, but cake tea dominated tribute and elite consumption. Salt, ginger, cinnamon and other flavourings were common; Lu Yu opposed “onion, ginger, jujube, orange peel, dogwood, mint,” saying they steal the true flavour. The debate marks a shift from medicinal flavouring towards plain drinking.

Tea and society

Tea entered monasteries: monks used it for meditation, hospitality and offerings; tea rites travelled to Japan with Buddhism. It entered literati life: Bai Juyi, Lu Tong and Jiaoran wrote tea poems linking tea to friendship, reclusion and clarity. It entered the court: tribute, gifts and tea banquets became institutions and ritual. It entered the frontier: the tea-horse trade began carrying tea to Tibet and the Uyghurs.

In 780 the Tang state began taxing tea, a sign that tea had become a fiscal object. Core regions lay in the Yangtze basin: Guzhu purple bamboo, Yangxian, Mengding and Jianzhou. Lu Yu said “Tang people rank Yangxian first,” but rankings shifted with tribute, fashion and literati taste; they were not a fixed league table.

Brewing and boundaries

Tang boiled tea cannot be faithfully reproduced with modern loose leaf: cake tea was softened, ground and sieved; water should be clear, light and flowing; salt should be minimal; powder should not boil too long. Modern attempts can use low salt, less powder and a short boil, but should not expect the freshness of today's green tea. Tang tea was stronger, bitterer, with salt and foam.

Tang tea is not the “ancestor of Japanese chanoyu” or the “origin of modern gongfu tea”; it is a separate system of cake tea ground and boiled. Lu Yu's Classic is not a national operating manual but a literati summary and standard; local ginger tea, gruel tea and loose tea persisted.
