Zhu Quan

Person

朱权（1378–1448）是明太祖朱元璋第十七子，封宁王，后改封南昌，自号臞仙、涵虚子。他经历宫廷斗争后转向道教、戏曲与茶事，著《茶谱》，在明初“末茶点饮”向“散叶撮泡”过渡中保留了点茶程序，也提出“林下一家生活”的文人茶境。

From prince to tea writer

Zhu Quan was the seventeenth son of the Ming founder and was enfeoffed as Prince Ning. He was based at Daning during the Hongwu era and later moved to Nanchang. After the political shifts of the early Ming, he turned to writing, music, drama and local gazetteers; the Tea Manual is one of these works. It argues for a simple, refined way of drinking tea, against elaborate grinding and whisking, and stresses leaf colour, aroma, taste and the elegance of vessels.

Where the Tea Manual sits

The Tea Manual appeared as cake tea was giving way to loose-leaf tea. It discusses tea products, utensils, water, drinking methods and the setting of a tea gathering, pulling tea from court and monastic ritual toward literati life. Its arguments carry personal choice and class taste and cannot be read as a national guide to early Ming drinking; they are nonetheless an important entry into Ming loose-leaf aesthetics.

Reading boundary

Zhu Quan was not a tea farmer, processor or modern tea scientist; his judgments belong to literati experience. Editions, compilations and later annotations affect the text. Quote the edition and section. Read Zhu Quan together with his political situation, regional experience and the purpose of tea writing, not as a collection of elegant phrases.

Prince and recluse

Zhu Quan was the 17th son of Zhu Yuanzhang, enfeoffed as Prince of Ning in 1391 at Daning (modern Ningcheng, Inner Mongolia); in 1403 he was moved to Nanchang. He held military power young, then lost in court struggles and turned to Daoism, opera, guqin and tea, calling himself Quxian, Hanxuzi and Danqiu.

Tea Manual and opera

He wrote Tea Manual and compiled Taihe Zhengyin Pu, a key early-Ming opera treatise. His tea book is not a “leisure note” but a disappointed prince’s spiritual outlet: tea is “a life under the trees,” a way to “scoff at the world,” and a Daoist cultivation medium.

Boundaries

Zhu Quan is not “the Tea Sage” or the only representative of Ming tea culture; his Manual compiles earlier texts and retains whisking, standing in transition. His life is not a romantic “retreat with tea”; court struggle, princely reduction and relocation are the background. Read the Manual by separating politics, Daoism and tea learning.
