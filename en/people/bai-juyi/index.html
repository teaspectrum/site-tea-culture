Person

Who he was

Bai Juyi (772–846), courtesy name Letian, styled Xiangshan Jushi, was a Tang poet and official from Taiyuan, born in Xinzheng, Henan. He was a jinshi in 800 and served in the palace secretariat, as a remonstrance official, as prefect of Jiangzhou and Hangzhou, and as an official in Luoyang. His poems are famous for plain language, clear narrative and social observation; “The Old Charcoal Seller” and “Song of Everlasting Sorrow” are standard examples. He also wrote a large body of tea poems, often tied to official travel, illness, friendship and retirement.

Tea in his life

Bai's tea poems are not a systematic tea manual. They record scenes: receiving tea sent by a friend, tasting spring water at a banquet, drinking alone at night, sending tea to a monk, planting tea on a mountain. Tea is a social gift, a marker of local culture and a way to settle the mind. The most quoted line is probably “I cannot forget the tea of the Rong region” from “Xie Li Liuxiang Ji Xin Cha” — an expression of taste memory, not a judgment about all tea.

How to read the tea poems

First separate “poem as historical document” from “poem as literary construction”. A poem can tell us that tea was sent as a gift and drunk at a gathering; it cannot by itself prove a processing method or a market price. Second, check the edition and title: some poems exist under variant titles, and some are attributed to Bai without firm evidence. Third, do not build a biography of “Bai the tea master” from a few lines; he was a poet who drank tea, not a tea professional.

Common misunderstandings

“Bai Juyi wrote a tea book” is false. “Bai Juyi was the first to write about tea” is also false; tea appears in earlier poetry. Some popular articles attribute a line about “tea from Mengding” to him without a source; check before quoting.

What he adds to tea culture

Bai shows tea entering the daily life of a Tang official and poet: not only in monasteries and tea-producing regions, but in offices, boats, illness and farewell. That everyday quality is more valuable than a list of famous teas. Read his tea poems alongside his biography and the geography of his postings, and the map of Tang tea culture becomes more concrete.

A note on editions

Bai's poems circulate in several collected editions, and titles sometimes vary. When a tea line is quoted online without a poem title or collection, treat it as unverified. For a first read, use a standard annotated selection and check the date and place of the poem; that context often explains why tea appears in the scene.

Source boundary
