Malaysian Teh Tarik

Custom

马来西亚拉茶（teh tarik）是浓红茶加炼乳、反复“拉”出泡沫的街头茶饮，常见于 mamak 摊和 kopitiam。它起源于印度移民社群，2024 年作为“马来西亚早餐文化”的一部分被列入 UNESCO 人类非遗名录。拉茶不是一种茶叶，而是配方、动作和社交场景的组合。

Foam made by pouring

Teh tarik is the pulled tea common in Malaysian and Singaporean kopitiams and mamak stalls. Strong black tea is mixed with condensed milk and poured repeatedly between two vessels, cooling the liquor and creating fine foam. A higher pour can make more foam, but the gesture is not the whole taste.

Tea base and sweetness

The base is usually a strong black tea; condensed milk supplies sweetness and body, and some recipes add evaporated milk or sugar. Strength and sweetness can be adjusted for the customer. In local usage “teh” usually means tea with milk, while “teh o” is the version without milk.

Reading boundary

Teh tarik belongs to street drinking and everyday social life, not traditional leaf appraisal. Foam, sweetness and tea strength differ from stall to stall. One recipe cannot represent every kopitiam.

Immigration and stalls

Teh tarik grew from Indian immigrant tea stalls (mamak). From the 19th century, South Indian migrants brought black tea, condensed milk and the “pull” to the Malay peninsula; the stall spread from labour communities to city streets and became a cross-ethnic daily drink. It is not an “ancient Malaysian tea” but a modern drink shaped by colonial trade, migrant labour and local taste.

Recipe and action

The typical recipe is strong black tea, condensed milk and sometimes evaporated milk; the tea is boiled strong, and condensed milk gives sweetness and body. “Pulling” pours the tea from one cup to another repeatedly, creating foam, cooling it and mixing milk and tea. A high, steady, unbroken pour is the vendor’s performance; fine foam shows good mixing, not “the more foam the better.”

Boundaries

Teh tarik is not a tea type or “milk-tea powder”; leaf, condensed-milk ratio and pull decide the drink. It often accompanies nasi lemak and roti canai as breakfast and all-day social drink. UNESCO’s 2024 listing is “breakfast culture in Malaysia,” not teh tarik alone; calling it the official “national drink” should be marked as a popular claim.
