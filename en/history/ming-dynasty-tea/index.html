Ming Dynasty Tea

History

明代茶事的关键转折是“废团改散”：洪武二十四年（1391）罢造龙团，采芽茶以进，散叶茶逐渐成为主流。炒青、撮泡、茶壶、茶盏和文人茶书共同塑造了明清至今的饮茶方式；茶从碾末点饮转向抓叶冲泡。

The key Ming shift was “abolish cake, promote leaf”: in 1391 the Hongwu emperor ordered an end to dragon cakes and required bud tea instead, and loose-leaf tea gradually became mainstream. Pan-firing, direct infusion, teapots, cups and literati tea books shaped tea drinking from the Ming-Qing to today; tea moved from powdered whisking to leaf infusion. The change was not overnight, but the direction was set.

Abolishing tribute cakes

Tang-Song mainstream was cake and powdered tea; the Ming court and commoners turned to loose leaf. Reasons were layered: cake production was laborious and tribute burdened farmers; pan-firing matured and gave more direct aroma; Zhu Yuanzhang, of humble origin, disliked luxury tribute and ordered “no more dragon cakes, only bud tea.” The order had political symbolism and a technical base.

“Abolish cake, promote leaf” does not mean “loose tea began in the Ming.” Tang and Song already had loose and coarse tea, just not mainstream; the Ming raised it to court and literati standard. Loose tea no longer needed grinding, sieving or whisking; it was put into a pot or cup and infused, and utensils shifted from grinder, sieve and whisk to teapot, cup and stove.

Pan-firing and direct infusion

Ming pan-firing matured: fixation, rolling and drying, with attention to wok temperature, hand movement and timing. Zhang Dai's essay on Min Wenshui describes tea “fired and cooled turn by turn”; Xu Cishu's Cha Shu says “turn quickly, take half-cooked as the measure.” Firing became a craft of controlling aroma and shape, not just drying.

Direct infusion means putting leaf into a pot or cup, pouring boiling water and steeping. It is simpler than whisking but depends more on leaf quality and water temperature. Ming tea books therefore discuss water, boil, utensils and setting; Yixing stoneware pots and white or celadon cups became central. Tea moved from “production performance” to “infusion art.”

Tea books and literati

Ming tea books multiplied: Zhu Quan's Tea Manual, Xu Cishu's Cha Shu, Zhang Yuan's Record of Tea, Luo Lin's Explanation of Tea, Wen Long's Notes on Tea. They no longer served only court tribute but literati, merchants and tea shops; content ran from processing and water to tea room, companions and utensils, a “life aesthetics” writing.

Literati combined tea with books, painting, incense, flowers and gardens; tea rooms, huts and stoves became reclusion spaces. Zhang Dai wrote of Min Wenshui and Wang Yuesheng, turning tea tasting into character judgement; Cha Shu wants a tea setting that is quiet, near water and away from vulgarity. Tea was not only a drink but identity, taste and social capital.

Industry and trade

Ming tea expanded in the domestic market; the tea-horse trade continued but was less central; overseas, tea moved with porcelain and silk to Japan, Southeast Asia and Europe. In the 17th century Dutch and English ships carried Chinese tea to Europe, and tea became a global commodity. The Ming was a key preparation for “Chinese tea going global.”

Ming tea law included monopoly, certificates and tribute; farmer burdens, private tea and border smuggling coexisted. Calling Ming tea only “literati leisure” ignores the economy and institutions; calling it only “tribute tea” ignores the loose-leaf market.

Brewing and boundaries

Ming direct infusion suits 80–90°C, 3–5 g to 150–200 ml, 1–2 minutes for the first steep; green tea should not be boiled or sealed too long, while oolong and black tea can take hotter water. Yixing pots suit oolong and pu'er; white porcelain gaiwan suits green, white and black tea and shows colour. The Ming had no modern “gongfu tea” concept, but direct infusion laid its base.

Ming tea is not “the absolute beginning of modern loose-leaf tea”; loose tea existed in Tang-Song, and the Ming made it mainstream. It did not “abolish all cake tea”; border brick tea and Tibetan tea continued. The Hongwu order targeted tribute, not a national ban on cakes.
