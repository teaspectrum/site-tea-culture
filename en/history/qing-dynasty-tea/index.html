Qing Dynasty Tea

History

清代茶事的主线是全球贸易与国内茶俗并行：广州十三行、武夷茶与红茶出口、鸦片与白银、印度茶竞争，以及功夫茶、茶馆、茶馆文化与边疆砖茶。19 世纪后期中国茶出口份额被印度、锡兰取代，茶业进入近代转型。

Qing tea ran on two tracks: global trade and domestic custom — the Canton system, Wuyi and black-tea exports, opium and silver, Indian competition, plus gongfu tea, teahouses and frontier brick tea. In the late 19th century China's export share was overtaken by India and Ceylon, and the industry entered a modern transition. The Qing was not simply “tea culture declining”; production, trade and consumption were all reorganised.

Canton and global tea trade

In 1757 the Qing court restricted foreign trade to Canton, and tea became a leading export. The British East India Company bought Wuyi tea, gongfu black tea and green tea, generating huge customs revenue in Britain and a silver drain. To balance trade, Britain smuggled opium into China, leading to the 1839–1842 Opium War and the Treaty of Nanjing.

After the five ports opened, Shanghai, Fuzhou and Xiamen expanded tea exports. In the 17th–19th centuries Chinese tea dominated world trade; Fujian Wuyi, Anhui Qimen, Zhejiang Pingshui and Hubei Yangloudong linked to global markets through ports and tea roads. Tea was not only a drink but a core of imperial finance, colonial trade and silver flows.

Black tea and Wuyi

Qing black-tea craft matured: Wuyi lapsang souchong and gongfu black tea (later Qimen, Dianhong) became export leaders. Lapsang used pine smoke, with smoky and longan notes; gongfu emphasised strip shape, fermentation and aroma. Black tea suited milk and sugar, matching the British market, and became the fastest-growing export category in the 18th–19th centuries.

Oolong developed in Fujian, Guangdong and Taiwan; Anxi Tieguanyin and Wuyi rock tea formed regional styles; green tea remained dominant at home. The Qing moved from “green tea first” to “green, black, oolong and dark tea coexisting,” laying the base for today's six categories.

Gongfu tea and teahouses

Southern Fujian and Chaozhou developed gongfu tea: small pots, small cups, high pour and low serving, “Guanyin patrols the city” and “Han Xin counts soldiers.” It stressed utensils, water, fire and rhythm, and is the direct source of today's gongfu practice. It was not a national Qing custom but a regional practice that travelled with migrants.

Teahouses multiplied in Qing cities: Beijing, Chengdu, Yangzhou and Guangdong tea houses served tea but also storytelling, opera, socialising, trade and mediation. They were public space and a crossroads of class, dialect and guild. Romanticising them as “old times” ignores their commercial and political functions.

Frontier and brick tea

Qing frontier tea continued: Hubei Yangloudong green brick, Hunan Anhua dark brick and Yunnan pu'er moved through the tea-horse road and the Ten Thousand Li Tea Road to Mongolia, Russia and Central Asia. Kyakhta border trade became the core of Sino-Russian tea trade in the 18th–19th centuries; brick tea was transportable, storable and essential to pastoral societies.

Tea moved on horses, camels and through Kyakhta markets, and was managed by the Qing border-sales system. Tea is not a single “Central Plains” story but a set of frontier trade networks.

Decline and transition

In the late 19th century India and Ceylon built large gardens with British capital and technology; CTC cut costs, and railways and steamships shortened transport. Chinese tea, hampered by smallholder production, taxes, uneven quality and war, lost export share. From the 1880s Indian tea displaced Chinese tea in the British market, and the industry entered a modern crisis.

Qing tea was not “straight decline”: domestic markets, frontier trade and local famous teas still grew; but global competition changed China's place. In the 20th century Wu Juenong and others pushed reform, opening the modern revival.

Brewing and boundaries

Qing gongfu suits oolong: 100°C, 7–8 g to a 100–120 ml small pot, rinse first, then 10–30 seconds, lengthening each infusion. Green and black tea follow their own temperatures; brick tea is boiled or long-steeped. The Qing had no temperature-controlled kettles; fire was judged by sound and steam.

Qing tea is not “only gongfu tea”; green, black, brick, teahouses and frontier trade coexisted. Nor was the Opium War “only about tea”; tea was one strand in a structure of opium, silver and colonial expansion. Calling Qing tea “closed and backward” ignores its global connections.
